A text for Engage 7 1999
Barny Drabble


'I'm interested in making connections with a general public as an individual 
living in a city... looking at that thing of being at once on your own and 
also part of a community or group.'

Anna Best


'I use myself as a symbol of how one individual operates within society or 
the world. Like tagging an elephant in the wild or something.'  

Ross Sinclair


    Two recent art events illustrate well how the practices of certain 
artists are challenging a whole range of assumptions surrounding the ever 
controversial process of introducing contemporary art to new audiences. 
Performance and process-based work are at present making new demands on the 
institutions, galleries and funding structures that choose to support them. 
When these structures are flexible enough the events that arise are providing 
unique experiences for the audiences who come in contact with them. 

The artist Ross Sinclair wears his concerns across his shoulders in the 
form of a gigantic tattoo of the words REAL LIFE, in a recent interview he 
explained:

'The tattoo reflects a lifetime's commitment to a project which aims to 
engage as wide an audience as possible in a debate surrounding the 
interesting gaps between life as it is lived by everyday people, and the 
aspirations afforded by spectacular life as we understand it through mediated 
images of the world.'  

At six o clock on the morning of June the 26th, the tattoo and Sinclair 
attached to it were on their way to Ridley Rd. Market In Dalston, Northeast 
London, where the regular traders were setting up their stalls for a hot and 
busy Saturday. Ridley Rd is one of London's largest and most multicultural 
street markets with stalls selling everything from fresh fruit and vegetables 
to musical greetings cards, boiler chickens and sari fabrics. 

It was in this tangibly 'authentic' environment that Sinclair chose to 
launch his own brand of 'I love Real Life' products. No stranger to building 
and performing in bizarre structures Sinclair spent the morning setting up a 
specially customised market stall, complete with a large red neon sign, hand 
painted banners, and brightly coloured boxes on which he displayed his 
product range. Coffee mugs, T-shirts, key rings, ballpens, badges and caps 
were available, all emblazoned with the logo 'I love Real Life'. By the time 
the first shoppers arrived he had all but disappeared into the hubbub and 
colour of the market, marked apart only by the tireless and surreal 
repetition of his message and the strange dysfunctional nature of his 
product. At one with the commercial and social purpose of the place Sinclair 
manned the stall for the day selling to those that would buy and talking to 
those that would talk.

In Kilburn across London a similarly unassuming series of interventions 
was taking place at the Mecca State Bingo Club. For Anna Best these were just 
one part of nearly a years work with Camden Arts Centre, looking at the 
experiences of Bingo devotees and exploring the strange parallels between 
gambling and religion. Where Sinclair's market stall was a consciously finite 
affair, Best sees her work at Mecca as 'a long slow trickle'  . As an artist 
most comfortable with the techniques of the researcher, it comes as a suprise 
that Best's real aims for the project were inherently supernatural, 'I 
wanted' she confesses 'to perform a miracle'  .

The project began with a consultation period, during which  short 
interviews with regular bingo players at Mecca were conducted by Best and her 
key collaborator Karen Stripp. The two jokingly became known by staff at the 
bingo hall as 'the therapists'. This period also saw regular discussions at  
Camden, involving amongst others the designer Thomas Andersson and Mecca's 
customer services manager, Shaun Fox. At both venues these were ideas 
sessions. At Mecca, players confided in the artists about their enjoyment of 
the game, their use of lucky charms, and in some cases, more sinisterly, 
about their addiction to gambling. At the Arts Centre too, talk wandered to 
people's personal encounters with luck, games, faith and rituals.

From the period of consultation came the structure for an event and the 
month of June was chosen for its numerical symmetry; thirty days, thirty 
numbers. Best chose specific images suggested by the interviews she had 
conducted and projected them daily in different locations around the Mecca 
building. Special Bingo events took place in Camden and Kilburn, including at 
one point the reversion of the Mecca building to its original purpose as a 
cinema. A short film interweaving Best's gathered images was accompanied by 
music played on the halls original Wurlitzer organ. For Best the project is 
not yet over, she is yet to perform the miracle she was hoping for and at 
present she is writing up this whole period of 'research' to form a 
publication.

With all public projects it is interesting to trace a line of agendas 
back from the artists to the curator, from the curator to the institution and 
from the institution to the funding body. Besides noting what a long distance 
it is from the policy-makers to these 'new audiences' they make policies 
for, it is also important to ask who in this line-up is listening to whom, 
and how the hopes for such initiatives at their conception in the boardroom 
differ from those at their delivery by the artist. 

Unusually for a public art project,'I love Real Life' was initiated by a 
commercial gallery, The Agency in Shoreditch. While planning a show of 
Sinclair's work at the gallery, the directors saw the opportunity to present 
a broader view of his practice and provide some publicity for both the show 
and the gallery. Sinclair was interested in placing art in a 'real' 
environment and seeing how it might function, while Dalston town centre 
manager Joanne Rogers saw that the event would bring new visitors to Ridley 
Road Market and provide something of interest for regular market-goers. With 
her support the project received funding through a local SRB grant.

The North London Link Project, of which Anna Best's work with Mecca is 
just one constituent, is funded through the Camden Arts Centre by Lottery 
money and the Henry Moore Foundation.  While the funding bodies are talking 
about 'greatly benefiting the public', the institution is talking about 
the 'potential for new audiences', the project coordinator is talking 
about 'trusting the artist' and the artist herself is adamant: 'I don't 
want to tailor something to a policy, this is my work'  . 

Somewhere between the beginning and end of these chains of influence 
there is a none too subtle change of agenda, from a picture of the artist as 
educator, do-gooder and ambassador for all things creative, to one of the 
artist as researcher, free agent, and ground-breaking subversive. The 
relationship that the latter picture has to wider audiences is notably less 
didactic, and more egalitarian. These artists require the assistance of a 
varied public to understand their own work, and not vice-versa. 

There are many kinds of artists working in the public sphere, just as 
there are many kinds of public art, what remains pertinent is to what extent 
the term 'commission' signals 'compromise' for artists who have their own 
reasons for working with non-art audiences. Peter Cross, coordinator of the 
London Link Project is quick to point out that with live and research based 
artwork like that of Sinclair and Best, this question of compromise is 
amplified. Where art-objects placed in public have a kind of 'like it or 
not' infallibility, art processes in the public sphere are a far riskier 
business. Best states this emphatically in relation to her work at Mecca:

'The artist in these kind of projects is in the 'front line', you can't do 
that without taking absolute responsibility for what you are doing, it has to 
be your work.' 

It is particularly notable that organisations who support more 
experimental interventions into the public space are going out on a limb to 
accommodate projects that offer none of the safety and familiarity of the by 
now ubiquitous exhibition / interpretation / education approach. Those 
working at The Agency and The Camden Arts Centre have had to show an often 
uncomfortable flexibility to organise projects like those in Dalston and 
Kilburn within their usual programme of on-site exhibitions. Things aren't 
always smooth, old ways of doing things don't dissolve over night and finding 
ways for measuring the success of these projects is far from easy. 
Importantly though, in spite of the fact that the whys and wherefores of 
Public Art may at times differ from policy makers to artists, the results of 
these projects and others like them around Britain can't help but stand up to 
the toughest scrutiny. 'Miraculous' or not, they are at the forefront of 
providing genuine, unsanitised, 'Real Life' exchanges between working 
artists and members of the general public.



Barny Drabble is a freelance curator and writer based in London. He worked 
with Ross Sinclair and The Agency gallery, curating the event 'I love Real 
Life' at Ridley Rd market. 
The author would like to thank Anna Best, Peter Cross, Joanne Rogers & Ross 
Sinclair.
